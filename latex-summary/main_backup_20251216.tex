\subsubsection{Qudits with canonical basis of Hamiltonians}
The canonical basis provides a complete orthogonal basis for $d\times d$ Hermitian matrices. For a $d$-dimensional Hilbert space, the basis operators are defined as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X_{jk} &= |j\rangle\langle k| + |k\rangle\langle j| \quad (j < k), \\
Y_{jk} &= -i|j\rangle\langle k| + i|k\rangle\langle j| \quad (j < k), \\
Z_j &= |j\rangle\langle j| - |j+1\rangle\langle j+1| \quad (j = 0, \ldots, d-2), \\
I &= \mathbb{1}_d,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:canonical_basis}
\end{equation}
where indices run over $j, k \in \{0, \ldots, d-1\}$. This yields $d^2$ operators total: $d(d-1)/2$ operators each for $X$ and $Y$ types, $(d-1)$ operators of $Z$ type, and the identity.

\textbf{Ensemble properties:} The canonical basis is characterized by extreme sparsity---each operator contains only 1--2 non-zero matrix elements, compared to $d^2$ for dense random matrices (GOE/GUE). To construct a parameterized Hamiltonian $H(\lambda) = \sum_{n=1}^{K} \lambda_n H_n$, we randomly sample $K$ operators uniformly without replacement from the canonical basis. This structured, deterministic ensemble models qudit systems with sparse, local Hamiltonians. The sparsity leads to discrete coverage of the Hilbert space, resulting in sharp reachability transitions at low control densities $\rho = K/d^2$, as observed in our numerical results. All figures in this work use the canonical basis ensemble unless stated otherwise.

 \subsubsection{Geometric 2-local Qubits (GEO2Local)} \label{sec:geo2}
The GEO2Local ensemble~\cite{https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06321v1} consists of geometrically 2-local Pauli operators on a graph $G = (V, E)$ with $n = |V|$ qubits. For a rectangular 2D lattice with $n = L_x \times L_y$ sites, the Hamiltonian is drawn from the Gaussian distribution
\begin{equation}
H(\mathbf{g}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{P}_2|}} \sum_{P \in \mathcal{P}_2} g_P P, \quad g_P \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1),
\label{eq:geo2local_general}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_2$ is the set of Pauli strings of locality at most $k=2$ (acting on at most 2 qubits within graph distance $\leq 2$), and $|\mathcal{P}_2|$ is the total operator count.

\textbf{Operator structure for $k=2$ on 2D lattice.} The set $\mathcal{P}_2$ contains:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{1-local terms}: $\{X_i, Y_i, Z_i\}$ for all sites $i \in V$, contributing $3n$ operators.
\item \emph{2-local terms}: $\{X_i X_j, X_i Y_j, \ldots, Z_i Z_j\}$ for all edges $(i,j) \in E$ with lattice distance $d_{\text{lattice}}(i,j) \leq 2$, contributing $9|E|$ operators.
\end{itemize}
The total operator count is $|\mathcal{P}_2| = L = 3n + 9|E|$, where $|E| = 2L_x L_y - L_x - L_y$ for a rectangular lattice with nearest-neighbor connectivity. For optimization, we reparameterize Eq.~\eqref{eq:geo2local_general} with control parameters $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^L$ instead of Gaussian coefficients, yielding
\begin{equation}
H(\lambda) = \sum_{i \in V} \sum_{\alpha \in \{X,Y,Z\}} \lambda_{i,\alpha} \sigma_i^\alpha
           + \sum_{\substack{(i,j) \in E \\ d(i,j) \leq 2}} \sum_{\alpha,\beta \in \{X,Y,Z\}} \lambda_{ij,\alpha\beta}
             \sigma_i^\alpha \otimes \sigma_j^\beta,
\label{eq:geo2local_k2}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^\alpha$ denotes the Pauli matrix for $\alpha \in \{X, Y, Z\}$.

\textbf{Example: Transverse-Field Ising Model.} The nearest-neighbor transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{TFIM}} = -J \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} Z_i Z_j - h \sum_i X_i
\end{equation}
is a special case of Eq.~\eqref{eq:geo2local_k2} with $k=1$ locality (nearest neighbors only), using only $ZZ$ and $X$ terms. The full GEO2Local($k=2$) ensemble includes all 2-local Pauli strings (e.g., $XY$, $YZ$, next-nearest $XX$, etc.), providing a much richer operator space than TFIM while maintaining geometric constraints.

\textbf{Qubit specificity and dimensional scaling.} The GEO2Local ensemble is inherently qubit-based, with each lattice site hosting a $d=2$ dimensional quantum system. The total Hilbert space dimension grows exponentially as $\dim(\mathcal{H}) = 2^{n}$ with lattice size $n = L_x \times L_y$. This contrasts fundamentally with the canonical basis ensemble (Section~\ref{sec:canonical}), which explores a \emph{single} qudit of dimension $d \in \{8, 10, \ldots, 16\}$ with Hilbert space dimension $\dim(\mathcal{H}) = d$. While both ensembles use control density $\rho = K/d^2$ in plots, the meaning of ``$d$'' differs: for canonical basis, $d$ is the qudit dimension; for GEO2Local, $d = 2^n$ is the exponentially-large total Hilbert space dimension. Consequently, GEO2Local and canonical basis explore complementary physical regimes: \emph{many weakly-coupled qubits under geometric constraints} versus \emph{a single strongly-interacting qudit}. Direct quantitative comparison of critical densities $\rho_c$ between ensembles is precluded by their fundamentally different dimensional structures.

\textbf{Computational complexity and quantum learning.} The GEO2Local ensemble was introduced in~\cite{https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06321v1} to study the complexity of $\varepsilon$-sampling and $\varepsilon$-emulation tasks under geometric constraints. Related work~\cite{https://arxiv.org/html/2312.07658v1} has established that sampling from output distributions of geometrically local Hamiltonians (including Ising, XX, and Heisenberg models) is classically intractable on 2D lattices. Our reachability analysis quantifies these limits: the critical control density $\rho_c(\tau)$ and threshold-dependent transition width $\Delta(\tau)$ determine the resource requirements (number of Hamiltonian terms $K$) needed to reach target states with fidelity $> \tau$. This directly informs the achievability of $\varepsilon$-approximation tasks, where $\varepsilon \sim 1 - \tau$, providing a state-space perspective on approximation capabilities of geometrically constrained Hamiltonians.

Consider an initial state $\ket{\phi}$ evolving under a Hamiltonian $H = \sum_{n=1}^{k} \lambda_n H_n$, where $\{H_1, \ldots, H_k\}$ is a fixed set of generators and the $\lambda_n$ are real parameters. The time evolution yields $\ket{\phi(t)} = \exp(-iHt)\ket{\phi}$. We call a state $\ket{\psi}$ \emph{reachable} from $\ket{\phi}$ if it lies in the closure of such orbits, meaning $\ket{\psi}$ can be approached arbitrarily closely by some $\ket{\phi(t)}$.\footnote{In finite dimensions, this accounts for situations analogous to irrational winding on a torus, where an orbit comes arbitrarily close to certain states without ever reaching them exactly. For instance, every state $\ket{\psi'} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{0} + e^{i\alpha}\ket{1} + e^{i\beta}\ket{2})$ is reachable from $\ket{\phi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\ket{0} + \ket{1} + \ket{2})$ with Hamiltonian $H = \ket{1}\bra{1} + \sqrt{2}\ket{2}\bra{2}$ in this sense, though only a measure-zero subset can be reached exactly.}

We denote the eigenstates of $H$ by $\ket{\alpha_i}$, with $H\ket{\alpha_i} = E_i\ket{\alpha_i}$. Generically, unless the generators $H_n$ possess special structure (such as shared symmetries), the only conserved quantities are functions of $H$ itself. Since $[H, f(H)] = 0$ for any function $f$, any such conserved quantity constrains which final states $\ket{\psi}$ are reachable. Checking whether a candidate state violates one of these conservation laws is often simpler than directly verifying reachability. Throughout this section, we write $\braket{A}_\alpha = \bra{\alpha}A\ket{\alpha}$ for expectation values.

We now present three criteria for deciding whether a state $\ket{\psi}$
 is reachable from $\ket{\phi}$ via a Hamiltonian drawn from a specified family.

\subsubsection{\ko{Moment criterion}}

Since the Hamiltonian commutes with its own exponential, all moments $\langle H^m \rangle$ are conserved under time evolution:
\begin{equation}
    \langle H^m \rangle_{\phi(t)} = \langle e^{iHt} H^m e^{-iHt} \rangle_\phi = \langle H^m \rangle_\phi.
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $\ket{\psi}$ is reachable from $\ket{\phi}$, the two states must share all moments of the Hamiltonian.\footnote{For states that lie on the orbit, this is immediate. For states in the closure of the orbit, conservation follows from continuity of the expectation value, which holds in finite dimensions.} The case $m=1$ corresponds to energy conservation.

Recall that we are interested in whether \emph{any} Hamiltonian from the family $H = \sum_n \lambda_n H_n$ can connect $\ket{\phi}$ to $\ket{\psi}$. Conservation of the first moment requires
\begin{equation}
    L := \sum_n \lambda_n \left( \langle H_n \rangle_\phi - \langle H_n \rangle_\psi \right) = 0.
\end{equation}
By itself, this constraint is not very useful: as long as some generators have $\langle H_n \rangle_\phi \neq \langle H_n \rangle_\psi$, we can always find weights $\lambda_n$ that make $L$ positive, negative, or zero. The first moment alone cannot rule out reachability.

The second moment provides a stronger constraint. Defining
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
    Q &:= \langle H^2 \rangle_\phi - \langle H^2 \rangle_\psi \\
    &= \sum_{n,m} \lambda_n \lambda_m \left( \langle \{H_n, H_m\} \rangle_\phi - \langle \{H_n, H_m\} \rangle_\psi \right),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\{A, B\} = AB + BA$ denotes the anticommutator, we require $Q = 0$ for any reachable state. As with $L$, this condition alone can often be satisfied by a suitable choice of weights. However, requiring \emph{both} $L = 0$ and $Q = 0$ simultaneously is more restrictive.

To formalize this, consider the quadratic form
\begin{equation}
    M(\alpha) = Q + \alpha L^2 = \sum_{n,m} M_{nm} \lambda_n \lambda_m,
\end{equation}
where $M_{nm}$ is a real symmetric matrix determined by $\alpha$ and the expectation values of generators and their anticommutators. If $M(\alpha)$ is strictly positive definite (or strictly negative definite) for some choice of $\alpha$, then no non-trivial weights $\lambda_n$ can make both $L$ and $Q$ vanish. Since the trivial choice $\lambda_1 = \cdots = \lambda_k = 0$ generates no dynamics, this certifies that $\ket{\psi}$ is unreachable from $\ket{\phi}$ using any Hamiltonian in the family.

This criterion is straightforward to apply: it requires only the expectation values of the generators $H_n$ and their anticommutators over the states of interest, followed by checking whether a matrix is positive definite.\footnote{The latter is numerically well-behaved, as eigenvalue computations are stable and the result is unambiguous unless eigenvalues are extremely close to zero.} The limitation is that the test is one-sided: it can certify unreachability, but passing the test does not guarantee that $\ket{\psi}$ is reachable. For more refined analysis, one can extend to higher moments or optimize over the Hamiltonian family directly, though such approaches are more computationally involved and offer weaker mathematical guarantees.

\subsubsection{\ko{Spectral overlap criterion}}
Any state that is not an eigenstate of the Hamiltonian will evolve, but its decomposition in terms of eigenstates changes only by phases. Fix a nondegenerate Hamiltonian $H$ with eigenstates $\ket{\alpha_i}$ and eigenvalues $E_i$, and decompose the initial state as
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\phi} = \sum_i \sqrt{p_i} \ket{\alpha_i},
\end{equation}
where we have absorbed any phases into the definition of the eigenstates. The probabilities $p_i = |\langle \alpha_i | \phi \rangle|^2$ are invariant under evolution generated by $H$.

For a target state $\ket{\psi} = \sum_i \sqrt{q_i} e^{i\theta_i} \ket{\alpha_i}$, the distance to the orbit of $\ket{\phi}$ is bounded from below\footnote{\textbf{[TODO: add proof]}}:
\begin{equation}
    \inf_{t \in \mathbb{R}} \| \ket{\phi(t)} - \ket{\psi} \| \geq C \sum_i |p_i - q_i|,
\end{equation}
for dimension-dependent constant $C > 0$. Reachability therefore requires $p_i = q_i$ for all $i$.

This motivates the spectral overlap criterion. Define
\begin{equation}
    S(\ket{\phi}, \ket{\psi}, H) = \sum_i \sqrt{p_i q_i} = \sum_i |\langle \psi | \alpha_i \rangle| \, |\langle \alpha_i | \phi \rangle|,
\end{equation}
which equals 1 if and only if the two probability distributions coincide. We numerically optimize $S$ over the Hamiltonian family $H = \sum_n \lambda_n H_n$. If the maximum value of $S$ falls below $1 - \varepsilon$ for a chosen tolerance $\varepsilon$, the state is deemed unreachable. \todoko{[TODO: current plots use $\varepsilon = 0.05$; reduce to $\varepsilon = 0.01$ , what are the typical thresholds in quantum control anyway?]}

Unlike the moment criterion, the spectral criterion requires numerical optimization over the parameters $\lambda_n$, which may converge to local maxima and does not provide a certificate of correctness. However, it offers a more fine-grained test: rather than checking polynomial constraints on expectation values, it directly examines whether the spectral distributions can be made to match. One advantage is that optimization is only over the Hamiltonian parameters, not over time, since the probabilities $p_i$ and $q_i$ are time-independent.

\subsubsection{\ko{Krylov criterion}}
The final criterion is similar in spirit to the spectral one: it requires numerical optimization over the Hamiltonian family, but avoids explicit diagonalization of $H$.

Given an initial state $\ket{\phi}$ and a fixed Hamiltonian $H$, the Krylov subspace is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K}(\ket{\phi}, H) = \mathrm{span}\{\ket{\phi}, H\ket{\phi}, H^2\ket{\phi}, \ldots\}.
\end{equation}
Since the Hilbert space is finite-dimensional, this sequence terminates at some dimension $s$. We represent the subspace by an orthonormal basis $\{\ket{\kappa_1}, \ldots, \ket{\kappa_s}\}$ constructed via successive orthogonalization, with $\ket{\kappa_1} = \ket{\phi}$ by convention.

The orbit of $\ket{\phi}$ under $H$ lies entirely within the Krylov subspace, since $e^{-iHt}\ket{\phi}$ is a power series in $H$ acting on $\ket{\phi}$. Therefore, reachability of a target state $\ket{\psi}$ requires $\ket{\psi} \in \mathcal{K}(\ket{\phi}, H)$. To test this, we construct the projector $P = \sum_{i=1}^{s} \ket{\kappa_i}\bra{\kappa_i}$ and define
\begin{equation}
    K(\ket{\phi}, \ket{\psi}, H) = \| P \ket{\psi} \|.
\end{equation}
If $\ket{\psi}$ belongs to the Krylov subspace, $K = 1$; otherwise $K < 1$.

As with the spectral criterion, we numerically optimize $K$ over the Hamiltonian family $H = \sum_n \lambda_n H_n$. If the maximum value falls below $1 - \varepsilon$, the state is deemed unreachable. The same caveats apply: optimization may find only local maxima and does not provide a certificate. \todoko{[TODO: ask Tomek about speed comparison with spectral criterion.]}

\textbf{Continuous Krylov score.} In our implementation, we employ a continuous variant of the Krylov criterion that avoids threshold-based unreachability decisions during optimization. For a parameterized Hamiltonian $H(\lambda) = \sum_{n=1}^{K} \lambda_n H_n$ and Krylov rank $m \leq d$, we construct the Krylov subspace $\mathcal{K}_m(H(\lambda), |\phi\rangle) = \text{span}\{|\phi\rangle, H(\lambda)|\phi\rangle, \ldots, H(\lambda)^{m-1}|\phi\rangle\}$ via Arnoldi iteration, yielding an orthonormal basis $V = [|q_0\rangle, |q_1\rangle, \ldots, |q_{m-1}\rangle]$. The continuous Krylov score is defined as
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{Krylov}}(\lambda) = \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} |\langle q_j | \psi \rangle|^2 = \|V^\dagger |\psi\rangle\|^2,
\end{equation}
measuring the projection of the target state $|\psi\rangle$ onto the Krylov subspace. Equivalently, $R(\lambda) = 1 - \|\epsilon_{\text{res}}(\lambda)\|^2$, where $\epsilon_{\text{res}} = |\psi\rangle - VV^\dagger |\psi\rangle$ is the projection residual. Optimization via L-BFGS-B yields $R^* = \max_{\lambda} R(\lambda)$; unreachability is declared if $R^* < \tau$ for a threshold $\tau \in [0,1]$. This formulation provides a smooth objective function suitable for gradient-based optimization and naturally generalizes to threshold-dependent unreachability analysis.

The continuous score $R_{\text{Krylov}}(\lambda)$ generalizes the discrete criterion $K = \|P|\psi\rangle\|$ described above. When the Krylov rank saturates at $m = s$ (the full Krylov dimension), the two formulations coincide: $R(\lambda) = K^2$ for fixed $\lambda$. The continuous variant optimizes over $\lambda \in [-1,1]^K$ with threshold $\tau$, whereas the discrete version applies a fixed threshold $1-\varepsilon$ to a single Hamiltonian. Our implementation exclusively uses the continuous formulation because (i) it avoids binary thresholding during optimization, yielding smoother gradients, and (ii) it naturally extends to threshold-dependent unreachability analysis, enabling direct comparison with the spectral criterion across varying $\tau$ values.

\subsection{Canonical Basis Ensemble: Unreachability Decay}

We investigate the scaling of unreachability probability $P(\rho)$ versus control density $\rho = K/d^2$ for the canonical basis ensemble across three reachability criteria. Figure~\ref{fig:final_summary} displays the primary results at threshold $\tau = 0.95$, while Figure~\ref{fig:tau_analysis} analyzes the dependence on both $\tau$ and dimension $d$.

\textbf{Universal vs dimension-dependent scaling.} The three criteria exhibit qualitatively distinct functional forms for critical control number $K_c(\tau)$:
\begin{align}
K_c^{\text{moment}} &= 4.2 \pm 0.1 \quad \text{($\tau$-independent)}, \label{eq:Kc_moment}\\
K_c^{\text{spectral}}(d,\tau) &= a(d) + b \tau, \quad b = 8.08 \pm 0.35 \text{ (universal)}, \label{eq:Kc_spectral}\\
K_c^{\text{Krylov}}(d,\tau) &= a(d) + b(d) \tau, \quad b \in [5.1, 9.5] \text{ (dimension-dependent)}. \label{eq:Kc_krylov}
\end{align}
The moment criterion's algebraic structure (anticommutator test) eliminates threshold dependence. The spectral criterion displays universal $\tau$-sensitivity: slope $b$ varies by only 4.3\% across $d \in \{10, 12, 14\}$, indicating that overlap maximization exhibits scale-invariant threshold scaling. Conversely, the Krylov criterion shows dimension-dependent slopes ($b = 5.1$ for $d=10$, $b = 9.5$ for $d=14$), reflecting increased optimization complexity in higher-dimensional Krylov subspaces. At threshold $\tau = 0.95$, spectral transitions occur at $K_c \in [10.3, 14.5]$ across dimensions, while Krylov transitions occur at $K_c \in [8.2, 12.1]$, demonstrating consistently lower control requirements for the Krylov test at this threshold.

\textbf{Transition sharpness and $\Delta(\tau)$.} Both spectral and Krylov criteria exhibit Fermi-Dirac transitions
\begin{equation}
P(\rho) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp[d^2(\rho - \rho_c)/\Delta]}, \label{eq:fermi_dirac}
\end{equation}
where the critical density $\rho_c$ and transition width $\Delta$ depend on threshold $\tau$ and dimension $d$. The critical control number scales as $K_c(\tau) = d^2 \rho_c(d,\tau)$, with density exhibiting universal $1/d$ scaling:
\begin{equation}
\rho_c(d,\tau) \approx \frac{K_c(\tau)}{d^2}, \quad K_c(\tau) = a(d) + b\tau \text{ (spectral, universal $b$)}, \label{eq:rho_c_scaling}
\end{equation}
or dimension-dependent slopes for Krylov. The transition width follows power-law scaling near perfect fidelity:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(\tau) \sim (1-\tau)^\beta, \quad \beta \approx 0.8 \text{--} 1.2 \text{ (ensemble-dependent)}. \label{eq:delta_scaling}
\end{equation}
Numerically, at $\tau = 0.95$, the Krylov criterion exhibits sharper transitions ($\Delta \approx 0.6$) than spectral ($\Delta \approx 1.0$), indicating tighter controllability boundaries for Krylov subspace tests. The coupled $\tau$-dependence of $K_c(\tau)$ and $\Delta(\tau)$ reflects a fundamental trade-off: higher fidelity thresholds demand both more control operators and produce sharper reachability transitions.

\textbf{Summary of scaling laws.} Our analysis reveals quantitatively distinct reachability phase transitions for the three criteria:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Moment:} $\tau$-independent algebraic threshold at $K_c^{\text{moment}} = 4.2 \pm 0.1$ (\ref{eq:Kc_moment}), reflecting anticommutator structure.
\item \textbf{Spectral:} Universal linear scaling $K_c^{\text{spectral}}(d,\tau) = a(d) + 8.08\tau$ (\ref{eq:Kc_spectral}) with 4.3\% coefficient variation across $d \in \{10, 12, 14\}$.
\item \textbf{Krylov:} Dimension-dependent slopes $K_c^{\text{Krylov}}(d,\tau) = a(d) + b(d)\tau$ (\ref{eq:Kc_krylov}) with $b \in [5.1, 9.5]$, indicating scale-dependent optimization complexity.
\item \textbf{Transition form:} Fermi-Dirac shape $P(\rho) = [1 + \exp(d^2(\rho - \rho_c)/\Delta)]^{-1}$ (\ref{eq:fermi_dirac}) with power-law width $\Delta(\tau) \sim (1-\tau)^\beta$ (\ref{eq:delta_scaling}).
\item \textbf{Density scaling:} All criteria exhibit $\rho_c \sim 1/d$ (\ref{eq:rho_c_scaling}), consistent with covering $d^2$-dimensional operator space.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Open questions.} Several avenues warrant further investigation:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Ensemble universality:} Do the qualitative scaling trends (universal spectral slope, dimension-dependent Krylov slopes) persist for dense random matrix ensembles (GOE/GUE) or geometric Hamiltonians (GEO2Local)?
\item \emph{Origin of universal spectral slope:} What geometric or algebraic property of spectral overlap maximization yields $\tau$-slope $b \approx 8.08$ independent of dimension?
\item \emph{Krylov dimension-dependence mechanism:} Why does Krylov subspace optimization exhibit increasing $\tau$-sensitivity ($b = 5.1 \to 9.5$) with dimension, while spectral remains constant?
\item \emph{Power-law exponent $\beta$:} Can the transition width exponent $\beta \approx 0.8$--$1.2$ in $\Delta \sim (1-\tau)^\beta$ be derived from random matrix theory or perturbation arguments near perfect controllability ($\tau \to 1$)?
\item \emph{GEO2Local reachability:} Does geometric locality on 2D qubit lattices produce sharper or smoother transitions than canonical basis ensembles? How do connectivity constraints affect $K_c(\tau)$ scaling?
\end{itemize}
